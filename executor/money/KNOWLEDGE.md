# 1. NATURE OF MONEY

## 1.1 What is Money?

**Money** is anything generally accepted as a medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value. It solves the "double coincidence of wants" problem in barter systems.

### Functions of Money
```
1. Medium of Exchange
   - Facilitates transactions
   - Eliminates barter inefficiency
   - Widely accepted

2. Unit of Account
   - Standard measure of value
   - Prices expressed in money
   - Enables comparison

3. Store of Value
   - Preserves purchasing power
   - Can be saved for future use
   - Wealth storage

4. Standard of Deferred Payment
   - Basis for credit/debt
   - Future obligations in money terms
```

## 1.2 Properties of Good Money

### Ideal Characteristics
```
Durability:       Doesn't wear out quickly
Portability:      Easy to carry
Divisibility:     Can be split into smaller units
Uniformity:       All units identical
Limited Supply:   Scarcity maintains value
Acceptability:    Widely recognized and trusted
Cognizability:    Easy to identify and verify
```

## 1.3 Types of Money

### Commodity Money
```
Definition: Money with intrinsic value

Examples:
  - Gold coins
  - Silver coins
  - Cattle
  - Salt
  - Shells

Properties:
  - Value comes from the commodity itself
  - Limited by natural supply
  - Costly to produce
```

### Representative Money
```
Definition: Claims on commodity money

Examples:
  - Gold certificates
  - Silver certificates
  - Warehouse receipts

Properties:
  - Backed by stored commodity
  - More portable than commodity
  - Redeemable for underlying asset
```

### Fiat Money
```
Definition: Money by government decree

Examples:
  - US Dollar
  - Euro
  - Japanese Yen
  - All modern currencies

Properties:
  - No intrinsic value
  - Legal tender by law
  - Value from trust in government
  - Supply controlled by central bank
```

### Bank Money (Credit Money)
```
Definition: Money created through lending

Examples:
  - Demand deposits
  - Checking accounts
  - Savings accounts

Properties:
  - Largest component of money supply
  - Created when banks make loans
  - Destroyed when loans repaid
```

### Digital Money
```
Definition: Electronic representation of value

Examples:
  - Cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum)
  - Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs)
  - Stablecoins
  - Digital wallets

Properties:
  - Exists only electronically
  - Programmable
  - Borderless (often)
```

## 1.4 Money vs Currency vs Wealth

### Distinctions
```
Money:
  - Broader concept
  - Includes all forms of exchange
  - Abstract economic function

Currency:
  - Physical or digital medium
  - Notes and coins
  - Specific form of money

Wealth:
  - Total value of assets
  - Includes non-money assets
  - Net worth measure
```

# 2. HISTORY OF MONEY

## 2.1 Pre-Money Exchange

### Barter System
```
Direct exchange of goods/services
Problems:
  - Double coincidence of wants
  - Indivisibility
  - No standard of value
  - Difficult storage
```

### Gift Economies
```
Reciprocal giving without immediate exchange
Based on social obligations
Still exists in some societies
```

## 2.2 Commodity Money Era

### Timeline
```
~9000 BCE: Cattle and grain as money
~3000 BCE: Silver bars in Mesopotamia
~1200 BCE: Cowrie shells in China
~700 BCE:  First coins (Lydia, modern Turkey)
~600 BCE:  Greek coins (drachma)
~500 BCE:  Chinese coins with holes
~300 BCE:  Roman denarius

Key Features:
  - Intrinsic value
  - Weight-based
  - Local standards
```

### Precious Metal Standards
```
Gold:
  - Scarce, durable, divisible
  - Universal appeal
  - Basis for international trade

Silver:
  - More common than gold
  - Used for smaller transactions
  - Bimetallic standards common
```

## 2.3 Paper Money Evolution

### China (First Paper Money)
```
~800 CE:  Tang Dynasty "flying cash"
~1000 CE: Song Dynasty official notes
1260 CE:  Yuan Dynasty paper money
1368 CE:  Ming Dynasty (overissue → inflation)
```

### Europe
```
1661: Stockholm Banco (Sweden) - first European banknotes
1694: Bank of England founded
1700s: Colonial currencies in America
1776: Continental Currency (Revolutionary War)
1792: US Mint established
```

## 2.4 Modern Monetary History

### Gold Standard Era (1870-1914)
```
Features:
  - Currency backed by gold
  - Fixed exchange rates
  - Free gold movement
  - Price stability

Classical Gold Standard:
  - £1 = 113 grains of gold
  - $1 = 23.22 grains of gold
  - £1 = $4.87 (fixed)
```

### Interwar Period (1918-1939)
```
1920s: Attempts to restore gold standard
1929: Great Depression begins
1931: UK abandons gold standard
1933: US confiscates gold, devalues dollar
1936: France abandons gold standard
```

### Bretton Woods System (1944-1971)
```
Features:
  - US dollar pegged to gold ($35/oz)
  - Other currencies pegged to dollar
  - IMF and World Bank created
  - Capital controls allowed

Problems:
  - Triffin Dilemma
  - US deficits
  - Gold outflows from US
```

### Nixon Shock (1971)
```
August 15, 1971:
  - US suspends gold convertibility
  - End of Bretton Woods
  - Beginning of fiat currency era

Results:
  - Floating exchange rates
  - Pure fiat currencies
  - Central bank independence
```

### Modern Era (1971-Present)
```
1970s: High inflation, stagflation
1980s: Volcker disinflation, financial deregulation
1990s: Globalization, emerging markets
2000s: Housing bubble, Global Financial Crisis
2010s: QE, zero interest rates
2020s: Pandemic stimulus, digital currencies
```

# 3. MONETARY SYSTEMS

## 3.1 Types of Monetary Systems

### Commodity Standard
```
Currency backed by physical commodity

Gold Standard:
  - Currency redeemable for gold
  - Fixed gold price
  - Gold reserves determine money supply

Silver Standard:
  - Currency backed by silver
  - Common before gold standard

Bimetallic Standard:
  - Both gold and silver
  - Fixed ratio between metals
  - Gresham's Law problems
```

### Fiat System
```
Currency by government declaration
No commodity backing
Value from:
  - Legal tender laws
  - Tax payment requirement
  - Trust in government
  - Central bank management
```

### Mixed Systems
```
Partial backing:
  - Some gold reserves
  - Not fully convertible

Currency boards:
  - Fixed to anchor currency
  - Full foreign reserve backing
  - Example: Hong Kong
```

## 3.2 Exchange Rate Regimes

### Fixed Exchange Rate
```
Features:
  - Government maintains rate
  - Intervention in forex markets
  - Reserves needed

Types:
  - Hard peg: Strict maintenance
  - Soft peg: Narrow band allowed
  - Crawling peg: Regular adjustments

Pros:
  - Price stability
  - Trade certainty
  - Inflation anchor

Cons:
  - Loss of monetary policy
  - Vulnerable to speculation
  - Requires reserves
```

### Floating Exchange Rate
```
Features:
  - Market-determined rate
  - No intervention
  - Free movement

Types:
  - Free float: No intervention
  - Managed float: Occasional intervention
  - Dirty float: Regular smoothing

Pros:
  - Monetary policy freedom
  - Automatic adjustment
  - No reserve requirement

Cons:
  - Exchange rate volatility
  - Trade uncertainty
  - Speculation
```

## 3.3 Monetary Unions

### Currency Union Characteristics
```
Single currency across countries
Common central bank
Examples:
  - Eurozone (€)
  - CFA franc zone (Africa)
  - East Caribbean Dollar
```

### Optimal Currency Area Theory
```
Criteria for successful union:
  - Labor mobility
  - Capital mobility
  - Synchronized business cycles
  - Fiscal transfers mechanism
  - Similar economic structures
```

### Eurozone
```
Members: 20 countries (as of 2023)
Central Bank: European Central Bank (ECB)
Currency: Euro (€)
Launched: 1999 (virtual), 2002 (physical)

Challenges:
  - No fiscal union
  - Asymmetric shocks
  - Divergent economies
```

# 4. CENTRAL BANKING

## 4.1 Central Bank Functions

### Core Functions
```
1. Monetary Policy
   - Set interest rates
   - Control money supply
   - Manage inflation

2. Financial Stability
   - Lender of last resort
   - Systemic risk oversight
   - Macroprudential regulation

3. Currency Issuance
   - Physical notes and coins
   - Digital currency (emerging)

4. Bank Supervision
   - Regulate commercial banks
   - Ensure soundness
   - Deposit insurance (often)

5. Payment Systems
   - Operate settlement systems
   - Clear interbank payments

6. Government's Bank
   - Hold government accounts
   - Manage public debt
   - Foreign reserves
```

## 4.2 Major Central Banks

### Federal Reserve (US)
```
Founded: 1913
Structure:
  - Board of Governors (7 members)
  - 12 Regional Reserve Banks
  - Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)

Dual Mandate:
  1. Maximum employment
  2. Stable prices (2% inflation target)
```

### European Central Bank
```
Founded: 1998
Structure:
  - Executive Board (6 members)
  - Governing Council (+ national governors)
  - General Council

Mandate:
  - Price stability (primary)
  - Support economic policy (secondary)
  - Inflation target: Below but close to 2%
```

### Bank of Japan
```
Founded: 1882
Independence: 1998

Mandate:
  - Price stability
  - Financial system stability
  - Known for unconventional policies
```

### Bank of England
```
Founded: 1694
Independence: 1997

Mandate:
  - Price stability (2% inflation)
  - Support government economic policy
```

### People's Bank of China
```
Founded: 1948
Status: Not fully independent

Functions:
  - Monetary policy
  - Exchange rate management
  - Financial regulation
  - Capital controls
```

## 4.3 Monetary Policy Tools

### Conventional Tools
```
Policy Interest Rate:
  - Fed Funds Rate (US)
  - Main Refinancing Rate (ECB)
  - Bank Rate (UK)

Open Market Operations:
  - Buy/sell government securities
  - Inject/withdraw liquidity

Reserve Requirements:
  - Minimum reserves banks must hold
  - Less used in modern policy

Discount Window:
  - Emergency lending to banks
  - Lender of last resort
```

### Unconventional Tools
```
Quantitative Easing (QE):
  - Large-scale asset purchases
  - Expand balance sheet
  - Lower long-term rates

Forward Guidance:
  - Communicate future intentions
  - Shape expectations
  - "Lower for longer"

Negative Interest Rates:
  - Below zero policy rate
  - Charge banks for deposits
  - Used by ECB, BOJ, SNB

Yield Curve Control:
  - Target specific yields
  - BOJ 10-year yield target

Credit Easing:
  - Targeted lending programs
  - Specific market support
```

## 4.4 Central Bank Balance Sheet

### Components
```
ASSETS                           LIABILITIES
─────────────────────           ─────────────────────
Government securities           Currency in circulation
Foreign reserves               Bank reserves
Loans to banks                 Government deposits
Gold                           Reverse repos
Other assets                   Capital

Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Capital
```

### Balance Sheet Expansion (QE)
```
Before QE:
  Assets: $800B
  Liabilities: $800B (currency + reserves)

After QE:
  Assets: $4.5T (bought $3.7T in securities)
  Liabilities: $4.5T (increased reserves)

Effect:
  - More reserves in banking system
  - Lower interest rates
  - Higher asset prices
```

## 5.1 Bank Functions

### Core Activities
```
Deposit Taking:
  - Demand deposits (checking)
  - Savings deposits
  - Time deposits (CDs)

Lending:
  - Consumer loans
  - Mortgages
  - Business loans
  - Credit cards

Payment Services:
  - Check clearing
  - Wire transfers
  - Debit cards
  - Bill payment
```

### Bank Balance Sheet
```
ASSETS                           LIABILITIES
─────────────────────           ─────────────────────
Cash & reserves                 Customer deposits
Government securities           Borrowed funds
Loans:                          Other liabilities
  - Mortgages
  - Commercial loans           SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY
  - Consumer loans             ─────────────────────
Other assets                   Common equity
                               Retained earnings

Key Ratios:
  Loan-to-Deposit: Loans / Deposits (< 100% typically)
  Reserve Ratio: Reserves / Deposits
  Capital Ratio: Capital / Risk-Weighted Assets
```

## 5.2 Bank Profitability

### Net Interest Margin (NIM)
```
NIM = (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets

Typical range: 2-4%

Factors:
  - Yield curve shape
  - Competition
  - Asset mix
  - Funding costs
```

### Fee Income
```
Sources:
  - Account fees
  - Transaction fees
  - Investment management
  - Insurance sales
  - Advisory services
  - Credit card fees
```

## 5.3 Bank Risks

### Credit Risk
```
Definition: Borrowers fail to repay

Measures:
  - Non-performing loan ratio
  - Loan loss provisions
  - Credit ratings

Management:
  - Diversification
  - Credit analysis
  - Collateral requirements
  - Loan covenants
```

### Liquidity Risk
```
Definition: Cannot meet obligations

Measures:
  - Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)
  - Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)

Management:
  - Liquid asset buffer
  - Diversified funding
  - Contingency plans
```

### Interest Rate Risk
```
Definition: Rate changes affect value

Duration Gap:
  - Asset duration vs liability duration
  - Mismatch creates risk

Management:
  - Duration matching
  - Interest rate swaps
  - Floating rate assets/liabilities
```

### Operational Risk
```
Definition: Failures in processes, people, systems

Examples:
  - Fraud
  - System failures
  - Compliance failures

Management:
  - Internal controls
  - Disaster recovery
  - Insurance
```

## 5.4 Bank Regulation

### Capital Requirements (Basel III)
```
Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1): 4.5%
Tier 1 Capital: 6%
Total Capital: 8%

Plus Buffers:
  - Conservation buffer: 2.5%
  - Countercyclical: 0-2.5%
  - G-SIB surcharge: 1-2.5%
```

### Liquidity Requirements
```
Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR):
  HQLA / Net cash outflows (30 days) ≥ 100%

Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR):
  Available stable funding / Required stable funding ≥ 100%
```

### Deposit Insurance
```
Purpose: Protect depositors, prevent runs

US (FDIC):
  - $250,000 per depositor per bank
  - Funded by bank premiums

Eurozone:
  - €100,000 per depositor per bank
  - National schemes
```

# 6. MONEY SUPPLY & CREATION

## 6.1 Money Supply Measures

### Monetary Aggregates
```
M0 (Monetary Base):
  = Currency in circulation + Bank reserves at central bank

M1 (Narrow Money):
  = Currency + Demand deposits + Other checkable deposits

M2 (Broad Money):
  = M1 + Savings deposits + Small time deposits + Money market funds

M3 (Even Broader):
  = M2 + Large time deposits + Institutional money market funds
```

### US Money Supply (2023 approx)
```
M0: ~$5.5 trillion
M1: ~$18 trillion
M2: ~$21 trillion

Ratio M2/M0 ≈ 4 (money multiplier effect)
```

## 6.2 Money Creation Process

### How Banks Create Money
```
Step 1: Initial Deposit
  Customer deposits $1,000 cash
  Bank: +$1,000 reserves, +$1,000 deposit liability

Step 2: Bank Lends
  Reserve requirement: 10% ($100)
  Bank can lend: $900
  When spent, $900 deposited at another bank

Step 3: Second Bank Lends
  Reserve requirement: $90
  Can lend: $810
  Process continues...

Final Result:
  Initial deposit: $1,000
  Total deposits created: $10,000 (1/10% = 10x)
  Total reserves: $1,000
```

### Money Multiplier
```
Simple Multiplier = 1 / Reserve Ratio

With 10% reserve ratio:
  Multiplier = 1 / 0.10 = 10

More Complex:
  m = (1 + c) / (c + r + e)

Where:
  c = Currency/Deposit ratio
  r = Reserve requirement
  e = Excess reserve ratio
```

### Endogenous Money Theory
```
Alternative view:
  - Banks create loans first
  - Then obtain reserves
  - Reserves accommodate lending
  - Central bank provides reserves on demand

Implications:
  - Money supply is demand-driven
  - Central bank controls price (interest rate)
  - Not quantity of money
```

## 6.3 Central Bank Money Creation

### Quantitative Easing
```
Process:
1. Central bank creates reserves (electronic)
2. Buys assets from banks/market
3. Pays with newly created reserves
4. Bank reserves increase
5. Asset prices rise

Not "Printing Money" in traditional sense:
  - No physical printing
  - Creates bank reserves
  - Doesn't directly enter economy
```

### Helicopter Money
```
Theoretical Concept:
  - Central bank gives money directly to public
  - Bypasses banking system
  - Pure monetary stimulus

Reality:
  - Requires fiscal-monetary coordination
  - Constitutional issues in some countries
  - COVID stimulus approached this
```

# 7. INTEREST RATES

## 7.1 Interest Rate Fundamentals

### What is Interest?
```
Price of borrowing money
Compensation for:
  - Time preference (delayed consumption)
  - Inflation risk
  - Default risk
  - Liquidity preference
```

### Types of Interest Rates
```
Nominal Rate:
  - Stated rate
  - Not adjusted for inflation

Real Rate:
  Real rate ≈ Nominal rate - Inflation

Fisher Equation:
  (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation)
```

### Key Interest Rates
```
Policy Rates:
  - Fed Funds Rate: 5.25-5.50% (2023)
  - ECB Deposit Rate: 4.00% (2023)
  - BOE Bank Rate: 5.25% (2023)

Interbank Rates:
  - SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate)
  - LIBOR (being phased out)
  - Euribor

Consumer Rates:
  - Prime rate: ~8.5%
  - Mortgage rates: ~7%
  - Credit card rates: ~20%
```

## 7.2 Yield Curve

### Definition
```
Relationship between interest rates and maturities

Normal Curve:
  Long rates > Short rates
  Upward sloping

Inverted Curve:
  Short rates > Long rates
  Recession predictor

Flat Curve:
  Similar rates across maturities
  Transition period
```

### Yield Curve Shapes
```
            Rate
             │
    Steep    │        ___________
             │      _/
             │    _/
             │  _/
    Normal   │ /
             │/
             └──────────────────── Maturity
                1Y  2Y  5Y  10Y  30Y

            Rate
             │
    Inverted │\
             │ \
             │  \_
             │    \_
             │      \_________
             │
             └──────────────────── Maturity
```

### Yield Curve Theories
```
Expectations Theory:
  Long rates = Average of expected future short rates

Liquidity Premium Theory:
  Long rates include premium for duration risk
  Explains normal upward slope

Market Segmentation:
  Different investors for different maturities
  Supply/demand in each segment
```

## 7.3 Interest Rate Determinants

### Factors Affecting Rates
```
Central Bank Policy:
  - Policy rate setting
  - Forward guidance
  - QE/QT

Inflation Expectations:
  - Higher expected inflation → Higher rates
  - TIPS spread measures this

Economic Growth:
  - Strong growth → Higher rates
  - Recession → Lower rates

Risk Appetite:
  - Risk-on → Higher risk-free rates
  - Risk-off → Flight to safety

Government Debt:
  - Higher debt → Higher rates (crowding out)
  - Supply/demand dynamics
```

## 7.4 Interest Rate Impact

### Economic Effects
```
Higher Rates:
  - Slower borrowing and spending
  - Stronger currency
  - Lower asset prices
  - Higher savings incentive

Lower Rates:
  - Stimulate borrowing
  - Weaker currency
  - Higher asset prices
  - Lower savings return
```

### Asset Price Impact
```
Bond Prices:
  ΔPrice ≈ -Duration × ΔYield

  Higher rates → Lower bond prices

Stock Prices:
  Present value of future cash flows decreases
  Higher discount rate → Lower valuations

Real Estate:
  Higher mortgage rates → Lower affordability
  Lower prices or slower appreciation
```

# 8. INFLATION & DEFLATION

## 8.1 Understanding Inflation

### Definition
```
Sustained increase in general price level
Decrease in purchasing power of money

Measured by:
  - CPI (Consumer Price Index)
  - PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures)
  - PPI (Producer Price Index)
  - GDP Deflator
```

### Inflation Calculation
```
Inflation Rate = (CPI_current - CPI_prior) / CPI_prior × 100

Example:
  CPI 2023: 304.7
  CPI 2022: 292.7
  Inflation = (304.7 - 292.7) / 292.7 × 100 = 4.1%
```

### Types of Inflation
```
Demand-Pull:
  - Too much money chasing too few goods
  - Economy overheating
  - Solution: Tighter monetary policy

Cost-Push:
  - Rising production costs
  - Supply shocks (oil, wages)
  - Harder to address

Built-In (Wage-Price Spiral):
  - Wages rise → Prices rise → Wages rise
  - Self-fulfilling expectations
```

## 8.2 Causes of Inflation

### Monetary Causes
```
Money Supply Growth:
  MV = PQ (Quantity Theory of Money)
  If M grows faster than Q → P rises

Central Bank Policy:
  - Low interest rates
  - Quantitative easing
  - Excessive money creation
```

### Non-Monetary Causes
```
Supply Shocks:
  - Oil price spikes
  - Natural disasters
  - Supply chain disruptions

Demand Shocks:
  - Fiscal stimulus
  - Consumer confidence
  - Government spending

Structural:
  - Demographics
  - Globalization (deflationary)
  - Technology (deflationary)
```

## 8.3 Effects of Inflation

### Winners and Losers
```
Winners:
  - Borrowers (pay back with cheaper money)
  - Asset owners (prices rise)
  - Government (inflates away debt)
  - Wage earners (if wages keep up)

Losers:
  - Savers (purchasing power erodes)
  - Lenders (repaid with cheaper money)
  - Fixed income recipients
  - Poor (spend higher % on necessities)
```

### Economic Costs
```
Shoe-Leather Costs:
  - Time spent managing cash
  - Minimizing money holdings

Menu Costs:
  - Cost of changing prices
  - Confusion and misallocation

Tax Distortions:
  - Bracket creep
  - Capital gains on inflation, not real gains

Uncertainty:
  - Harder to plan
  - Higher risk premiums
```

## 8.4 Deflation

### Definition
```
Sustained decrease in general price level
Increase in purchasing power of money
Often worse than moderate inflation
```

### Causes
```
Demand Collapse:
  - Great Depression
  - Japan's lost decades
  - Financial crises

Productivity Gains:
  - Technology
  - Good deflation (debatable)

Debt Deflation Spiral:
  - Falling prices
  - Real debt burden rises
  - Defaults increase
  - Asset prices fall further
```

### Why Central Banks Fear Deflation
```
Problems:
  - Consumers delay purchases
  - Real interest rates rise (can't cut below zero)
  - Debt burden increases
  - Expectations become entrenched

Japan Example:
  - Deflation since 1990s
  - Lost decades of growth
  - Zero/negative rates unsuccessful
```

## 8.5 Inflation Targeting

### Framework
```
Central Bank Sets Target:
  - Usually 2% annually
  - Symmetric (above and below)
  - Medium-term focus

Communication:
  - Announce target publicly
  - Explain deviations
  - Forward guidance
```

### Inflation-Targeting Central Banks
```
Country    Target    Since
─────────────────────────────
US (Fed)    2%       2012
ECB         2%       2003
UK          2%       1992
Canada      2%       1991
Australia   2-3%     1993
```

# 9. FOREIGN EXCHANGE

## 9.1 Forex Basics

### Exchange Rate Definition
```
Price of one currency in terms of another

Direct Quote: Domestic per foreign
  USD/EUR = 1.10 (US perspective)

Indirect Quote: Foreign per domestic
  EUR/USD = 0.91 (US perspective)
```

### Currency Pairs
```
Major Pairs:
  EUR/USD: "Euro"
  USD/JPY: "Dollar-Yen"
  GBP/USD: "Cable"
  USD/CHF: "Swissy"
  AUD/USD: "Aussie"
  USD/CAD: "Loonie"

Cross Rates:
  EUR/JPY, GBP/JPY, EUR/GBP

Exotic Pairs:
  USD/TRY, USD/ZAR, USD/MXN
```

## 9.2 Forex Market

### Market Characteristics

```
Size: $7.5 trillion daily (2022)
Hours: 24/5 (Sun 5pm - Fri 5pm ET)
Centers: London (largest), NY, Tokyo, Sydney

Participants:
  - Banks (interbank market)
  - Central banks
  - Corporations
  - Hedge funds
  - Retail traders
```

### Market Sessions
```
Session        Hours (ET)      Key Currency

```

## 9.3 Exchange Rate Determination

### Factors Affecting Rates
```
Interest Rate Differentials:
  Higher rates → Currency appreciation
  Attract capital inflows

Inflation Differentials:
  Higher inflation → Currency depreciation
  Purchasing power parity

Trade Balance:
  Trade surplus → Currency appreciation
  Demand for currency to buy exports

Capital Flows:
  Foreign investment inflows → Appreciation

Political Stability:
  Stability → Appreciation
  Risk → Depreciation (risk-off)
```

### Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)
```
Absolute PPP:
  S = P_domestic / P_foreign
  Exchange rate = Price level ratio

Relative PPP:
  ΔS/S = π_domestic - π_foreign
  Currency change = Inflation differential

Big Mac Index:
  Informal PPP measure
  Compare burger prices globally
```

### Interest Rate Parity
```
Covered Interest Rate Parity:
  F/S = (1 + r_domestic) / (1 + r_foreign)

Forward rate reflects interest differential

Uncovered Interest Rate Parity:
  E(S_future)/S = (1 + r_domestic) / (1 + r_foreign)

Expected spot rate reflects differential
```

## 9.4 Currency Trading

### Spot vs Forward
```
Spot:
  - Immediate exchange
  - Settlement T+2
  - Current market rate

Forward:
  - Future exchange
  - Lock in rate today
  - Used for hedging
```

### Currency Derivatives
```
Futures:
  - Exchange-traded forwards
  - Standardized contracts
  - CME main venue

Options:
  - Right but not obligation
  - Calls and puts
  - Used for hedging and speculation

Swaps:
  - Exchange currencies + interest
  - Common for corporations
  - Manage currency exposure
```

### Mortgage Math
```
Monthly Payment:
  P = L[c(1+c)^n]/[(1+c)^n-1]

  P = Monthly payment
  L = Loan amount
  c = Monthly interest rate
  n = Number of payments

Example:

  c = 0.065/12 = 0.00542
  n = 360
  P = $2,528/month
```

# 20. PSYCHOLOGY OF MONEY

## 20.1 Behavioral Biases

### Cognitive Biases
```
Loss Aversion:
  - Losses hurt 2x more than gains feel good
  - Hold losers too long
  - Sell winners too early

Overconfidence:
  - Overestimate ability
  - Trade too much
  - Take excessive risk

Anchoring:
  - Fixate on arbitrary numbers
  - Purchase price anchor
  - Affects selling decisions

Recency Bias:
  - Weight recent events heavily
  - Performance chasing
  - Panic selling
```

### Emotional Biases
```
Fear:
  - Sell during downturns
  - Miss recovery

Greed:
  - Chase returns
  - Excessive risk
  - Ignore warning signs

Regret:
  - Avoid decisions
  - Or, make impulsive ones
```

## 20.2 Money Scripts

### Types of Money Scripts
```
Money Avoidance:
  - "Rich people are greedy"
  - Leads to self-sabotage

Money Worship:
  - "More money will solve everything"
  - Never satisfied

Money Status:
  - "Self-worth = Net worth"
  - Overspending to impress

Money Vigilance:
  - "Don't discuss money"
  - Can lead to healthy saving or anxiety
```

### Identifying Your Scripts
```
Childhood experiences
Family money messages
Cultural influences
Significant money events
```

## 20.3 Spending Psychology

### Hedonic Adaptation
```
New purchases provide temporary happiness
Baseline returns quickly
Experiences > Things (often)
Relationships matter more
```

### Lifestyle Inflation
```
Spending rises with income
"Keeping up with the Joneses"
Combat: Save raises first
Define "enough"
```

## 20.4 Building Healthy Money Habits

### Key Principles
```
1. Automate good behavior
   - Auto-save, auto-invest
   - Remove willpower from equation

2. Know your "why"
   - Values-based spending
   - Goals as motivation

3. Focus on what you can control
   - Savings rate
   - Asset allocation
   - Costs and fees

4. Long-term perspective
   - Ignore short-term noise
   - Time in market beats timing

5. Continuous learning
   - Financial education
   - Adapt as life changes
```

### Common Mistakes to Avoid
```
- Timing the market
- Chasing performance
- Ignoring fees
- Not diversifying
- Checking accounts too often
- Making emotional decisions
- Not starting early enough
- Trying to get rich quick
```
